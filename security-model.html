Security Model

Who can see what — made obvious
D365 Prep

Think of one rental company. Who gets to see which reservations?

That one question is the security model. We'll use the same little company the whole way down — a head office with two branches — and build every piece on top of it, slowly. Play with the controls; don't just read.

Start with this and everything else clicks: every permission is really two questions stuck together — what are you allowed to do, and whose records can you do it to?
QUESTION 1
What can you do?
Create, Read, Write, Delete, Append, Append To, Assign, Share. This is a privilege.
QUESTION 2
Whose records?
Just mine? My branch? My branch + below? Everyone? This is the access level (depth).

A security role is just a big grid that answers both questions for every table. Let's make Question 2 — the confusing one — physical.

The 5 access levels, in plain words

These are the little circle icons you see in the real role editor — emptier = less reach, fuller = more. Same order, every table:

None — nothing. · User (Basic) — only records you own (or that were shared with you, or your team's). · Business Unit (Local) — any record owned by someone in your branch. · Parent: Child (Deep) — your branch and every branch beneath it. · Organization (Global) — every record, any branch.
Two rules that save you: roles are additive — you get the union of every role you hold (your own + each team's), and the least-restrictive one wins. There is no "deny" — you can't take access away with a second role (column security is the one exception, later).

Now the four ways to stretch access further

Roles + business units cover most of it. These four add reach on top — each solves a real problem the branch story runs into.

Teams — three kinds, and when to use each

A team is a group of users. Because roles are additive, a team can hold a role and every member borrows it. Three flavours:

  • Owner team — can own records (one owner per record) and holds roles. Good for modelling a department that owns its data.
  • Access team — can't own anything; it shares specific records with its members automatically. Built for dynamic, per-record collaboration and scales far better than hand-sharing.
  • Entra ID (Azure AD) group team — membership is synced from an Azure AD group, so IT manages it centrally.
PREXA365: a high-value reservation gets an access team so the assigned technician and the account manager can both edit it — without changing who owns it. Enterprise: a big sales deal gets an access team for its virtual pursuit team; departments are modelled as owner teams.
Sharing (POA) — the toggle you just used

Sharing grants one specific person (or team) access to one specific record — exactly what the "Share R-103" toggle did: Calgary's record lit up for a Toronto user even though their role would never reach it.

Behind the scenes every share is a row in the Principal Object Access (POA) table. That's the catch: shares pile up. Thousands of manual shares bloat POA and slow the system.

Rule of thumb: solve access with roles and teams first; use direct sharing only for the odd exception, not as your main design.
Hierarchy security — managers see their reports' records

Problem: a manager shouldn't need Organization access just to see their own team's work. Hierarchy security fixes that — it quietly grants a manager access to the records of the people below them.

  • Manager hierarchy — uses the manager field; a manager reaches their direct and indirect reports' records, limited by a depth you set.
  • Position hierarchy — same idea, but based on a Position record instead of the direct manager chain (useful when access shouldn't follow the literal line manager).
Enterprise: a regional sales manager automatically sees every rep's opportunities beneath them — no broad role needed, no manual sharing.
Column (field) security — hide one sensitive field

Everything so far works at the record level — can you see the row or not. But sometimes people should see the record yet not one field on it.

Mark that field Field Security = enabled, then grant Read / Update / Create per Field Security Profile to the people who may see it. Everyone else sees •••••.

PREXA365: the asset's cost / margin field is visible to managers only. Enterprise: a contact's bank account field is restricted to finance.
This is the one exception to "no deny": a secured field is hidden by default and only a profile opens it — access is subtractive here, unlike roles.
How it all adds up (say this out loud)
My access to a record = the widest of all my roles (mine + every team's) + anything shared with me + anything hierarchy gives me as a manager. It only ever adds up — the one place access is taken away is a secured column.
The whole model in one breath: a role answers what (privilege) and whose (access level); business units are the boundary those levels measure against; teams, sharing and hierarchy stretch reach further; and column security is the single subtractive piece.
